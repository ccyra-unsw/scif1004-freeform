01 · DISPATCH

Welcome aboard

Long before our crew selection began, you'd already watched astronauts race around space and struggle with survival a doze time over, in cinemas, on laptops, half-paying attention on a plane. Without meaning to, these films built the shape of what you believe Mars survival looks like, long before mission planners ever explained what it actually takes.

This handbook exists to close the gap, where each section starts with a piece of science that is central to surviving the transit and the planet itself. This is the same science thats embedded in the films you grew up on.

04 · INSTRUMENT

Radiation dose calculator

Radiation has a strange relationship with cinema. When it shows up on the screen, it's rarely the villain. Instead, a dose of gamma rays creates the Hulk. Cosmic rays gives the Fantastic Four their powers. Peter Parker was bit by a radioactive spider becoming spiderman. Radiation is what turned an ordinary person into something extraordinary.

When it comes to radiation on a trip to Mars, it works a lot differently. It's a slow invisible and cumulative cost that adds up over the transit. This is likely why it rarely makes it into a space movie script, as theres no specific frame that could capture its effects. Drag to set your transit time and see what it actually costs you.

05 · INSTRUMENT

Signal delay simulator

Interstellar uses signal delay as a large plot point. Rather than showing real-time talking across space, the film stretches communication delay to the most extreme possible case. Relativistic time dilation, where minutes for one character becomes years for another, and messages arrive as recorded videos from a life that's already moved on. It highlights that real-time conversation with Earth isn't physically possible on a space mission, and it won't be to Mars either.

06 · LIFE SUPPORT

The science
that actually keeps you alive

Survival in space comes down to a short list of problems: air, water, food, and a body that wasn't built for zero gravity. None of these make for a dramatic single scene the way a wormhole or a debris field does, but all three films you've been briefed on so far end up circling back to them anyway, sometimes as the whole plot, sometimes as a detail quietly running in the background.

Breathable air

In Gravity, oxygen is a countdown. Every suit has a limited supply, and the tension of the film rests on watching that number fall. Now this prompts the question, how do you keep producing air for years, rather than just carrying a tank of it? Mars offers no help here. Its atmosphere is almost entirely carbon dioxide, and less than 1% the pressure of Earth's, unbreathable and barely there. The real answer is a piece of chemistry NASA has already tested: MOXIE, an experimental unit aboard the Perseverance rover, has demonstrated splitting Martian CO₂ into oxygen and carbon monoxide at small scale, effectively manufacturing breathable air out of a planet's worth of the one thing it has in abundance.

Water and food

Interstellar opens with a version of this problem at planetary scale: Earth's crops are failing one species at a time, and the search for a new home is really a search for somewhere food can grow again. The Martian shrinks that same problem down to one person and one habitat. Watney's potato farm, grown in Martian regolith and fertilised with his own waste, works as both a survival plan and the clearest illustration either film gives of agriculture as a life-or-death engineering problem rather than a background detail. Growing anything in Martian soil is harder than the farm makes it look, the regolith contains perchlorate compounds that are toxic to humans and would need to be processed out before any crop grown in it was safe to eat. Water carries its own version of the same constraint: nothing can be wasted. Systems already running on the ISS recycle over 90% of onboard water, filtering it from sources as unglamorous as sweat, breath condensation, and urine, since on a multi-year mission, there's no version of "running out" you can recover from.

Gravity and the human body

The human body evolved under constant gravitational load, and without it, bone density and muscle mass begin to decline almost immediately, astronauts on long ISS missions can lose bone density at roughly ten times the rate of age-related loss on Earth. Interstellar gestures at a solution with the Endurance spins, using centripetal force to simulate gravity along its outer ring, the same principle that keeps water in a bucket you swing overhead. The physics behind it is sound and has been studied seriously for decades. What hasn't happened yet is building one. No crewed spacecraft has ever flown with a rotating section large enough to generate meaningful artificial gravity, which makes the Endurance less a depiction of current spaceflight than a preview of an engineering problem still waiting to be solved.

FAST FACTS
  • A person needs roughly 550 to 840 grams of oxygen a day to survive, meaning a life support system has to run without failure for years at a time.
  • Long-duration microgravity exposure can reduce bone density by up to 1 to 1.5% per month, around ten times faster than typical age-related bone loss on Earth.
  • Rotating a spacecraft to generate artificial gravity through centripetal force, as shown aboard Interstellar's Endurance, is scientifically sound in principle. No crewed spacecraft has ever actually built one.
  • Closed-loop systems like the ISS's must recycle water, scrub carbon dioxide, and manage oxygen simultaneously, with no single part allowed to fail.

None of these problems are visually dramatic on their own, which is part of why they rarely anchor a scene the way a wormhole or a storm does.
