Radiation dose calculator

Radiation has a strange relationship with cinema. When it shows up on the screen, it's rarely the villain. Instead, a dose of gamma rays creates the Hulk. Cosmic rays gives the Fantastic Four their powers. Peter Parker was bit by a radioactive spider becoming spiderman. Radiation is what turned an ordinary person into something extraordinary.

When it comes to radiation on a trip to Mars, it works a bit differently. It's a slow invisible and cumulative threat that adds up over the transit. Signal delay simulator

Interstellar uses signal delay as a large plot point. Rather than showing real-time talking across space, the film stretches communication delay to the most extreme possible case. Relativistic time dilation, where minutes for one character becomes years for another, and messages arrive as recorded videos from a life that's already moved on. It highlights that real-time conversation with Earth isn't physically possible on a space mission, and it won't be to Mars either.

06 · LIFE SUPPORT

The science
that actually keeps you alive

Survival in space comes down to a short list of problems: air, water, food, and a body that wasn't built for zero gravity. All three films you've been briefed on so far end up use these points, sometimes as the whole plot, sometimes as a detail quietly running in the background.

Breathable air

MARS ATMOSPHERE: <1% EARTH PRESSURE, ~96% CO₂

In Gravity, oxygen is a countdown. Every suit has a limited supply, and the tension of the film rests on watching that number fall. This prompts the question, how do you keep producing air for years, rather than just carrying a tank of it? Mars has an atmosphere that's almost entirely carbon dioxide, and is less than 1% the pressure of Earth's.

The answer is a piece of chemistry NASA has been testing: MOXIE, an experimental unit aboard the Perseverance rover. It has demonstrated splitting the CO₂ from Mars into oxygen and carbon monoxide at small scale, effectively manufacturing breathable air from the planet.

Water and food

ISS WATER RECYCLED: 90%+  ·  MARS SOIL: TOXIC UNTIL PROCESSED

Interstellar's opening scene has this problem where Earth's crops can no longer survive and there's a search for a new home somewhere food can grow again. Similarly, in The Martian, Watney's potato farm grown in Martian regolith and fertilised with his own waste, works as both a survival plan a realistic illustration of agriculture in film. Growing anything in Martian soil is harder than the movie makes it look, the regolith contains perchlorate compounds that are toxic to humans and would ned to be processed out before any crop grown in it was safe to eat.

Additionally, water has the constraint where nothing can be wasted. Systems already running on the ISS recycle over 90% of onboard water, filtering it from sources including sweat, breath condensation, and urine, since on a multi-year mission, there's no version of "running out" you can recover from.

Gravity and the human body

BONE LOSS IN MICROGRAVITY: UP TO 1.5% PER MONTH

The human body has evolved under a constant gravitational load. Without this, bone density and muscle mass both begin to decline almost immediately. Astronatus that are on long ISS missions, can lose bone density approximately ten times the rate of regular humans on Earth.

Interstellar suggests a solutions of Endurance spins, using centripetal force to simulate gravity along its outer ring (ie. when you swing a bucket of water over your head). These physics have been studied for decades yet no spacecraft has ever flown with a rotating section that was large enough to generate artificial gravity. This highlights the Endurace as an engineering problem still waiting to be solved.

FAST FACTS
  • A person needs roughly 550 to 840 grams of oxygen a day to survive, meaning a life support system has to run without failure for years at a time.
  • Long-duration microgravity exposure can reduce bone density by up to 1 to 1.5% per month, around ten times faster than typical age-related bone loss on Earth.
  • Rotating a spacecraft to generate artificial gravity through centripetal force, as shown aboard Interstellar's Endurance, is scientifically sound in principle. No crewed spacecraft has ever actually built one.
  • Closed-loop systems like the ISS's must recycle water, scrub carbon dioxide, and manage oxygen simultaneously, with no single part allowed to fail.
07 · SYNTHESIS

How cinema shapes
our understanding of space

You've now seen how three films, The Martian, Interstellar, and Gravity, each encounter a similar set of survival problems some of which includes: what to pack, radiation, communication, breathing, food, water and gravity. What each story does differently is how they are told. It demonstrates how cinema doesn't just depict space as a means for exploration. Instead, it shapes how we think about what matters when surviving out there.

What Hollywood emphasizes

Consider how each film treats the passing of time. Gravity compresses an orbital disaster into 90 minutes, turning survival into a sprint. The Martian stretches it across seasons, making survival a war of attrition against Martian geology and the need to farm. Interstellar weaponizes time itself: relativity becomes a character with its own agenda, where minutes cost years.

Each of these timescales serves the story being told: compressed, stretched, or bent depending on what the plot needs from time itself. A real Mars mission would likely feel closer to The Martian's grinding patience in its pacing, with an isolation closer to Interstellar's in its emotional weight. Gravity's pure crisis structure is the outlier: in deep space, problems tend to unfold slowly enough to think through, precisely because nobody is coming to help any faster than that.

On screen, off screen

All three films gravitate toward the same kind of moment: crisis, discovery, decision. They're drawn to it because it's the kind of moment a camera can hold onto, something changing, something at stake.

MAKES THE CUT

  • Orbital debris strikes and near-misses
  • A storm strong enough to abandon a mission
  • A wormhole, a wall of ice, a black hole
  • The moment a plan works, or fails

RUNS QUIETLY OFF-SCREEN

  • Life support, running for years without failure
  • Daily exercise to protect bone density
  • Sleep, on a fixed and monotonous schedule
  • Waiting, for weeks, with nothing to report

That stretch of quiet time isn't missing from these films by accident. Drama needs change, and this is the part of a mission where nothing visibly changes at all.

The feedback loop

Cinema doesn't just reflect how we think about space, it influences how space agencies plan for it, and how the public understands the risk involved. The first Mars missions will be designed partly by engineers who grew up watching these films.

The Martian's potato farm
→
A reference point in real agricultural research on off-world crops
Interstellar's rotating Endurance
→
Renewed public interest in a decades-old centripetal gravity concept
Gravity's cascading debris field
→
Wider awareness of a real, growing orbital debris problem

Cinema is built to show you the moments worth watching. Knowing which moments those are, and which ones simply run quietly in the background keeping you alive, is worth carrying with you before departure.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Film and reality operate on different timescales and different rules. Hollywood compresses drama into moments; space exploration is defined by what happens between the moments. Knowing the difference isn't about dismissing cinema. It's about seeing through it clearly enough to survive what it didn't show you.
